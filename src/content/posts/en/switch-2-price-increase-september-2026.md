There is a normal sequence to console pricing, and every platform holder has followed it for thirty years. You launch high, you sell to the people who were always going to buy on day one, and then as manufacturing costs fall you cut the price to reach everyone else. Sony has done it. Microsoft has done it. Nintendo has done it, repeatedly.

On 1 September, Nintendo is doing the opposite. The Switch 2 goes from $450 to $500 in the United States, with equivalent rises in Canada and Europe. Japan's increase already happened earlier this year.

What makes this genuinely unusual is not the increase on its own — tariffs and component shortages have pushed hardware prices around plenty of times lately. It is the timing against Nintendo's own numbers, which landed two weeks earlier and were not good.

## The numbers behind the decision

In the quarter ending 30 June 2026, Nintendo sold **3.82 million Switch 2 units, down 34.4% year on year**. Lifetime sales reached 23.68 million.

The year-on-year comparison deserves a caveat, and Nintendo will make it if you ask: the equivalent quarter in 2025 contained the console's launch, and launch quarters are always inflated by pent-up demand and channel fill. A drop from that base was always coming.

But a third is a large drop even accounting for that, and the rest of the release tells you where Nintendo's business is actually holding up:

| Metric | Q1 FY2027 | Change YoY |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Switch 2 hardware | 3.82m units | −34.4% |
| Switch 2 software | 9.46m units | +9.2% |
| Original Switch software | 33.81m units | +38.6% |
| Digital sales | ¥132.7bn | +90% |
| IP-related revenue | — | +107.4% |

Read that table again, because it is the whole story. Hardware is falling. Everything attached to hardware is climbing, some of it steeply. Original Switch software is up nearly 39% eight years into that console's life — people are still buying games for a machine Nintendo is theoretically replacing. Digital is now 61.5% of software revenue. And IP revenue doubled, almost entirely on the back of the Super Mario Galaxy film.

Nintendo is making more money from people who already own its hardware than it is from selling new hardware. That is a comfortable position, and it is precisely the position that lets a company raise a console price without immediately panicking.

## Why it costs more to build

The stated reason is components, and specifically memory. Nintendo first signalled a possible increase in [May 2026](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/05/08/nintendo-switch-2-price-hike-sales-fall-memory-crunch.html), citing the memory-chip crunch that has been squeezing the entire electronics industry.

This part is real and it is not Nintendo-specific. DRAM and NAND contract prices have been climbing as AI datacentre buildouts consume an enormous share of global fabrication capacity. A games console is a memory-heavy device competing for the same wafers as an AI accelerator, and it loses that fight on margin every time. Every hardware manufacturer is dealing with the same squeeze.

What differs is the response. The console industry's standard move is to eat the cost, because consoles are a razor-and-blades business — you take a thin margin or a loss on the box to grow the installed base, then earn it back on software, subscriptions and storefront cuts for the next six years. Under that logic, raising the price of the razor while you are still building the base is close to heresy.

Nintendo has never fully subscribed to that logic. It has historically refused to sell hardware at a loss, and it is not starting now. Given the software and digital figures above, it is also the platform holder least exposed to the downside: if higher prices slow adoption, the existing 23.68 million Switch 2 owners and 156.59 million Switch owners keep buying games regardless.

## What it means if you were going to buy one

If a Switch 2 was already in your plans for this year, buy it before 1 September. That is $50, there is no hardware revision on the horizon, and Nintendo has never cut a console's launch price inside its first eighteen months. Waiting for a discount is a bad bet on this specific company.

If you were undecided, the price change tells you nothing useful about the console. It is the same machine on 2 September as it was on 31 August. The question worth asking is the software one, and there the answer has improved recently: **Splatoon Raiders**, the series' PvE departure, launched on 23 July and sold [474,684 copies in Japan in its first week](https://nintendoeverything.com/famitsu-sales-7-20-26-7-26-26-first-week-sales-revealed-for-splatoon-raiders-big-nintendo-switch-2-boost/), more than doubling Switch 2 hardware sales there in the same week — from 24,928 units to 50,168.

That is the mechanic Nintendo is betting on. One system-seller does more for a console than fifty dollars ever will, in either direction.

## The number to watch

Nintendo has **held its full-year forecast at 16.5 million Switch 2 units**. It has sold 3.82 million of those. That leaves roughly 12.7 million consoles to move in nine months, through a holiday quarter, at a price fifty dollars higher than the one that produced a 34% decline.

Management could have quietly trimmed the forecast alongside the price rise and nobody would have been surprised. Choosing not to is a statement: Nintendo is telling investors it believes the back half of the year — holiday, plus whatever is still unannounced — carries the number on its own.

The next earnings report is where we find out whether that was confidence or stubbornness. Those look identical right up until they don't.
